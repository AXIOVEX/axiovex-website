## Program one — Projected constraints

**Section heading:** Program one — Projected constraints

**Intro:** Most of science describes the world as *stuff* — particles,
fields, forces — and then asks what the stuff does. This program
starts one step earlier. It describes a system by the **limits** on
it: the rules about what can happen and what cannot. Then it asks a
simple question with surprising reach — if all we ever get to see is
what those limits allow through, what does the world have to look
like? Internally, the program is called **RCPH**. Everything it
claims is registered publicly before the test runs, and the result
is published either way — including when the answer is "we were
wrong."

### What we have learned so far

**Time is a pattern, not a metronome.** We built systems with no
clock in them at all — no time variable anywhere in the description —
and the pattern we call time showed up anyway, in the way the
system's records are organized. Time, in this picture, is something
the records *do*, not a background tick everything dances to. It
behaves the way a summary behaves: a compressed way of presenting a
structure that does not itself unfold. One more finding, seen again
and again in our tests: when these systems change, they tend to
**flip, not slide** — a state holds, holds, holds, and then switches,
rather than melting gradually from one form into the next.

**Gravity may be counting.** Take any region and draw its boundary —
a fence. Now count the connections that cross the fence. The count
grows with the *size of the fence*, not with the amount of stuff
inside it. That is exactly how gravity behaves in the real world:
its strength at a boundary depends on area, not volume, and nobody
has ever fully explained why. In our framework the area pattern
falls out of pure counting. Here is the honest catch, and we state
it as plainly as the result: the counting argument needs one extra
rule — a limit on how much a boundary can carry. We tried to derive
that rule from the framework's basic assumptions, and we could not.
So we did the next best thing: we **proved**, with the proof checked
by computer, that the rule genuinely does not follow from those
assumptions. It is not a hidden step in our math. It is an adopted
premise, listed openly, and anyone who wants to challenge the
gravity result knows exactly which premise to aim at.

**Everything may have started by un-squishing.** Our models of the
early universe start from a small, compressed state. We found that
a **single decompression** — one un-squishing, unfolding into the
structured state we observe — is enough. The universe does not need
to bounce or cycle forever to produce what we see. One detail turned
out to matter enormously: the compressed starting state has to be
an *ordinary* one, a typical example of its kind. Pick a special,
hand-tuned starting state and the result falls apart. Ordinariness
is load-bearing.

**Seeing is a report — and reports can be tested.** Part of this
program studies perception: what happens in the brain when a person
sees something faintly, at the edge of awareness, and says "I saw
it" — or doesn't. That report side of seeing is testable, and we
test it in registered studies on public brain-imaging data. Our
first strong idea about it failed its test, publicly, and we
retired it. But there is a second side — what seeing *feels like*
from the inside — and our work marks a hard boundary there: no
record, of any kind, in any experiment, can carry the feeling
itself, because records are exactly the things that get reported,
stored, and read back. That is not an excuse for not studying it.
It is a result about what kind of question it is.

**Why some questions stay stuck.** After dozens of tests, the
failures fell into three piles, and the piles turned out to be more
interesting than most of the successes. A question can stay stuck
because the thing it asks about is **already maxed out** — like
asking whether a full glass can get fuller. It can stay stuck
because the answer **isn't in the record** — the window we are
allowed to look through was never given that page. Or it can stay
stuck because the signal is **too faint to measure** — written
down, but below the floor of what any instrument can read.
Gravity's extra rule, the feeling side of seeing, the faint
perception signals — each of our open questions now has a named
wall it is facing. Knowing which wall you are up against is not
the same as getting through it, but it beats not knowing.

### What the program has learned so far

- Whether a quantum system's record texture survives depends on
  its coherences staying intact — not on how strongly its dynamics
  mixes. Rescaling the mixing changes nothing; removing the
  coherences ends the record.
- A counting law for how much information fits inside a boundary
  holds exactly in the model. The extra condition it needs from
  nature is still open — and we proved the current axioms cannot
  settle it either way.
- Two operations that sound alike — averaging away fine detail,
  and treating symmetric states as identical — are provably not
  the same operation. One of them is licensed by the axioms; the
  other is not.
- For a standard test of whether a noisy quantum channel can be
  undone, we mapped exactly where it fails as noise grows — and
  found a second kind of noise that repairs the failure instead of
  deepening it.
- A preregistered test of a decision signal in brain recordings
  closed without a verdict: the signal could not be read from that
  dataset at the standard fixed in advance. The claim stands
  unjudged, not falsified.

**Status line:** One further study is designed, registered, and
waiting on a laboratory partner.

Every study is registered in advance on the Open Science Framework,
with its test and its failure condition written down before the
computation starts. The registrations and the verdicts are public —
see the links at the bottom of this page.

## The framework, in plain terms

**Section heading:** The framework — projected constraints

**Intro:** "Projected constraints" is the name of the framework
behind Program one. It has five working ideas. None of them needs
mathematics to state, and every one of them is doing real work in
the results above.

**1. Start with limits, not stuff.** A constraint is a rule about
what cannot happen — a wall, a budget, a conservation law. Describe
a system by its constraints and you have described everything that
could ever be observed about it, without committing to what it is
"made of" underneath. When the usual description of a thing keeps
failing, it is often because the thing was never the right level to
describe. The limits were.

**2. Every view comes through an interface.** Nobody observes a
system directly. We observe it through a boundary — an instrument, a
sense organ, a dataset — and the boundary decides what can be asked
and what can be answered. The framework calls that boundary an
*interface*, and it treats the choice of interface as part of the
description, never as a detail. Change the interface and the same
underlying system presents a different visible world. A good deal
of confusion, in science and elsewhere, is two people arguing about
two different interfaces as if they were arguing about the world.

**3. Records are all we ever get.** A record is anything that
persists and can be read back: a measurement, a photograph, a
memory, a data file, a fossil. Every fact anyone has ever checked
arrived as a record. So the framework studies records directly —
what a record can contain, what it cannot contain no matter how it
is made, and what follows from that. The boundary around the
feeling side of seeing, above, is one such result: it is a theorem
about records before it is a claim about minds.

**4. Counting is unforgiving — and that is its power.** Many of
the framework's results come from counting: how many connections
cross a boundary, how many arrangements satisfy a set of
constraints, how many distinct things an interface can report.
Counting does not care what we hope is true. It is how the area
pattern behind gravity appears, and it is also how several of our
own favorite ideas died — the count said no, in public, and that
was the end of them.

**5. The method: register, run, publish — either way.** Every
claim in this program is handled the same way. First, state the
claim and the exact test that could kill it, and register both
publicly, before any computation runs. Second, run exactly what
was registered — no quiet adjustments afterward. Third, publish
the verdict whichever way it lands; a falsified claim is listed
with the same prominence as a confirmed one. Fourth, have the
work audited by reviewers who did not do it. And underneath all of
it, the formal mathematics is kept in a form a computer can check,
so an argument cannot quietly drift away from its own assumptions.
We are loyal to the test, not to the idea. An idea that fails is
retired, in public, and the program moves on to a better one.

## Program two — The epistemic ledger

**Section heading:** Program two — The epistemic ledger

**Intro:** The second program is about evidence itself. In most
fields, a claim's credibility lives in people's heads and in the
reputations of the people repeating it. The epistemic ledger gives
a claim a public, revisable **grade** instead: a score that goes up
when evidence survives testing and down when it fails, with the
reasons for every change written next to it, in the open, where
anyone can argue with them. A grade is never a verdict from
authority — it is a running summary of what the evidence has done
so far, and it is allowed to move.

The framework behind the ledger — why grades should move the way
they do, and what keeps the bookkeeping honest — is published as a
framing paper, and the ledger itself is maintained in a public
repository (link below). The same grading discipline runs
underneath Program one: it is how we keep score on our own claims,
and it is why every result above is stated with its mechanism and
its reasons attached, never as a bare count.

**Status line:** Framing paper published; ledger public and
maintained; the method is in active use across both programs.
