## 01 · HEADLINE — The claim, and the number it buried (0:46)

**Key points**

- Robots and AI together reach about 81% of U.S. job tasks — measured in tasks and working time, not jobs
- Robots are already cheaper than people for just 0.3% of job tasks
- The gap between what robots can do and what they cost is the study's real story

**Takeaway:** Capability is not the headline. Cost is.

**Narration**

A widely shared LinkedIn post claimed that AI and robots together can already perform tasks covering about eighty-one percent of U.S. employment. We read the study behind it, published September thirtieth by Anthropic's economics team. The summary's numbers are mostly right. Its unit is wrong. And the study's most useful figure, for anyone who runs a plant or plans a workforce, is the one the summary buried: zero point three percent. That is the share of job tasks for which a robot is already cheaper than the person doing them. The distance between what robots can do and what they cost is the whole story.

## 02 · THE MAP — What the study actually measured (1:04)

**Key points**

- About 900 occupations and 19,000 tasks scored; 7,594 of the tasks are physical
- Three settings: purpose-built, structured facilities, and unstructured — very little qualifies in the open world
- 54% of U.S. work time is cognitive or interpersonal; 46% is physical

**Takeaway:** Where a task happens decides whether a robot can do it at all.

**Narration**

The researchers started from O-Net, the federal occupational database: roughly nine hundred occupations, nineteen thousand task descriptions. Seven thousand five hundred ninety-four of the tasks are physical, and each was scored on whether a robot available today could perform it, and in what setting. A robot, here, means an autonomous machine that senses and acts on its own. The scoring used three settings: purpose-built environments, built for robots, like a factory assembly line; structured facilities, organized human workplaces, like a logistics warehouse; and unstructured settings, out in the open world, like a city road. One fact frames everything else: fifty-four percent of U.S. work time is cognitive or interpersonal, and forty-six percent is physical. Even a perfect score on physical work touches less than half the economy's hours.

## 03 · EXPOSURE — Tasks and hours, not jobs (1:08)

**Key points**

- The 81% counts tasks weighted by working time — not jobs, not workers
- Robots can do 74% of physical work — 34% of all working hours — mostly in purpose-built settings; only about 2% in the open world
- Workers in robot-exposed occupations earn about $30 an hour less, on average, than workers in unexposed ones

**Takeaway:** A job counts as exposed if a slice of its tasks is. That is not a robot doing the job.

**Narration**

What does the eighty-one percent actually cover? It is a share of job tasks, weighted by working time — not a share of jobs, workers, or employment. About half of all work time is exposed to language models alone — but only in theory: a language model could at least halve the time the task takes. It is not what AI is doing in workplaces today. Robots account for the rest. They can perform seventy-four percent of physical work — equal to thirty-four percent of all working hours. But roughly half of that physical work is doable only in purpose-built settings, about twenty-two percent in structured facilities, and only about two percent in unstructured settings. Most of that assumes the workplace was built or organized around the robot. And the exposure is not shared evenly: workers in robot-exposed occupations are paid roughly thirty dollars an hour less, on average, than workers in unexposed ones.

## 04 · THE GAP — Capable is not the same as cheaper (1:19)

**Key points**

- Cost-competitive means the full robot cost — purchase, installation, maintenance, supervision — beats human labor cost: 0.3% of tasks today
- Reaching 10% needs robot costs to fall about 70% — around 40 years at the 3%-a-year historical trend
- The full welder's job costs an estimated five times a human welder, even though the weld itself is doable

**Takeaway:** The weld was never the bottleneck. Everything around it is.

**Narration**

The second half prices the capability. A task counts as cost-competitive when a robot's full annual cost — purchase and installation spread over about ten years, plus maintenance, energy, insurance, and part-time human supervision — comes in below human labor cost. On that test, robots are cost-competitive for zero point three percent of job tasks today. For that share to reach ten percent, robot costs would need to fall about seventy percent. At the historical trend of roughly three percent a year since the nineteen-nineties, that takes around forty years. Cost-competitiveness for half of today's physical work arrives around twenty eighty-five on the baseline trend, and around twenty fifty if costs fall up to four times faster. Take welding: the weld itself is doable, and AI-powered welding cells are already deployed. But automating the full welder's job — positioning large parts, climbing to hard-to-reach joints, checking quality, grinding and finishing — costs an estimated five times what a human welder costs.

## 05 · WHERE IT PAYS — One occupation already clears the bar (1:12)

**Key points**

- Packers and packagers: about 560,000 U.S. workers, spending 97% of their time on tasks robots can do
- A multi-robot system priced at about $45,000 per worker per year already beats $49,000 for human labor
- Transportation and material-moving tasks jump from under 15% exposed to about 90% once robots are counted

**Takeaway:** The leading edge is not a wave. It is one occupation where a structured task met a cost line.

**Narration**

One large occupation clears the bar today: packers and packagers — about five hundred sixty thousand U.S. workers — spend ninety-seven percent of their time on tasks robots can do. The study prices a multi-robot system — over two million dollars to buy and install, replacing roughly fourteen workers' annual output — at about forty-five thousand dollars per worker per year, against forty-nine thousand for human labor. Already about two thousand five hundred dollars per worker per year cheaper. Employment in the occupation has fallen twenty-two percent since twenty fifteen. Movement is the other hotspot: transportation and material-moving tasks go from under fifteen percent exposed to about ninety percent once robots are counted, and nine of the ten most-exposed occupations are vehicle operators. And zero point three percent is not nobody: it still covers roughly three hundred thousand workers, in occupations where robots can do about ninety-five percent of the work.

## 06 · FINE PRINT — Read it with both eyes open (1:08)

**Key points**

- Every score was produced by Claude, Anthropic's own AI model; the main text describes no human rating step, and the study is not peer reviewed
- Anthropic's own earlier work found 94% theoretical AI coverage against 33% observed in actual usage data
- Capability barriers block about 70% of physical tasks — handling objects is the standout; planning limits block about 8%

**Takeaway:** The binding constraint is not intelligence. It is hands.

**Narration**

Now the cautions. Every score in it was produced by Claude, Anthropic's own AI model. Claude classified the tasks, found and cited the robot evidence, rated the exposure, and built the cost estimates. The study's main text describes no human rating or validation step, and the work has not been peer reviewed. That does not make it wrong — but it is a map drawn by a company selling one of the technologies being mapped. Anthropic's earlier work supplies the sharpest check: computer and mathematical occupations scored ninety-four percent on theoretical coverage, and thirty-three percent on what usage data actually showed. There is no equivalent usage data for robots. One finding matters for planning: capability barriers block about seventy percent of physical tasks, and the standout is manipulation — touching and handling objects. Planning and reasoning limits block only about eight percent.

## 07 · CLOSE TO HOME — What it means in Michigan (1:20)

**Key points**

- The study publishes no Michigan figures — this chapter is Axiovex's reading of it, labeled as such
- For a plant, the environment decides what is automatable sooner than wages do: layout, fixturing, part presentation, changeover
- Packing and structured material movement are near cost parity now; dexterous, judgment-heavy work is the work to hire and keep

**Takeaway:** Capability, cost, and observed use are three different numbers. Plan on the right one.

**Narration**

The study is national, with no Michigan figures — this last part is our reading, labeled as such. For a Michigan plant, exposure is a property of the environment, not the headcount: layout, fixturing, part presentation, and changeover discipline decide what is automatable sooner than wages do. Its ordering is a sensible capital sequence: packing and structured material movement are at or near cost parity now. Purpose-built line tasks are capable but not yet cheap — five-to-twenty-year conversations, not next year's budget. Dexterous, judgment-heavy work — setup, quality calls, exception handling, repair — is what robots do worst, and exactly the work to hire, train, and retain for. We are watching three things: whether researchers replicate the index with human raters; whether real robot costs bend faster than three percent a year; and whether the next occupations over the cost line are the ones the study points to. Capability, cost, and observed use are three different numbers, and a plan built on the wrong one is worse than no plan at all.
