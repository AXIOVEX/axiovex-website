On December 17, 2026, the last current-generation Cadillac CT5 will come off the line at GM's Lansing Grand River Assembly plant. The next one — a new generation, confirmed for the same plant — is not expected until the second half of 2028. Between those two dates sits roughly **18 months with no car to build**, **350 workers** facing layoff effective January 14, 2027, and a **$1.25 billion** retooling program. Coverage of the announcement has mostly argued about whether the plant is "closing." It is not. The harder and more useful question is what an 18-month hole in a plant's calendar actually requires — from GM, from workers living on reduced pay, and from the suppliers whose order books have the same hole in them.

## What is actually happening

The facts, from the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) filing — the formal layoff notice federal law requires — as reported by the Detroit News and the [Lansing State Journal](https://www.lansingstatejournal.com/story/money/business/2026/08/14/gm-layoff-cadillac-ct5-sedan-lansing-grand-river-plant/91307869007/):

- **350 employees** at Lansing Grand River Assembly and Lansing Regional Stamping will be laid off effective **January 14, 2027**, as the plants are retooled for future production including the next-generation, gasoline-powered CT5 ([GM Authority](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2026/08/gm-laying-off-350-workers-at-lansing-grand-river-plant-in-january-2027/)).
- The layoffs are **temporary**. GM expects to call employees back after the changeover, with **some returning before the end of 2027** — well ahead of the production restart.
- Affected employees may be eligible for transfers to other GM facilities, or for **supplemental unemployment pay equivalent to 74% of their hourly wage** under the national GM–UAW contract, per GM spokesperson Kevin Kelly.
- The CT5 is the plant's last remaining vehicle. The Chevrolet Camaro ended production there in December 2023 (369 employees were laid off at that time), and the Cadillac CT4 ended in June 2026.

Three corrections to the coverage are worth making plainly, because each one changes what the gap means:

**This is not a closure, and "18 months idle" is an estimate, not a GM statement.** GM has confirmed the next CT5 for Lansing Grand River. What GM has *not* published is a day-by-day account of the retool — when work starts, whether every part of the complex stops at once, or whether the stamping plant takes on other work in the meantime. [GM Authority's own follow-up](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2026/10/will-the-gm-lansing-grand-river-plant-really-be-closed-for-18-months/) says the same: the duration alone does not establish that the whole facility sits idle for the full period, and GM's expectation that some workers return during 2027 points the other way.

**The $1.25 billion was redirected, not scrapped.** The investment was first announced in 2023 to convert Lansing Grand River to electric-vehicle production, backed by a $500 million federal grant in 2024. EV demand came in below plan, and GM redirected the program to the gasoline CT5. One widely read account described the money as "allocated… but the whole thing was scrapped." That is backwards: the retooling now underway *is* the $1.25 billion program, aimed at a different vehicle.

**The 74% is a benefit, not a paycheck.** It is supplemental unemployment pay under the union contract — support paid to eligible laid-off workers, generally alongside state unemployment benefits — not GM continuing to pay 74% of wages as salary, and it applies to workers who are laid off rather than transferred. The difference matters for the household math below: benefits have eligibility rules and time limits that a paycheck does not.

## The timeline is the story

A production gap is a planning object: a set of fixed dates with decision points between them. Lansing's looks like this:

| Date | What happens |
|---|---|
| Dec 17, 2026 | Current CT5 production ends. The plant's last vehicle is done. |
| Jan 14, 2027 | Layoffs take effect: 350 workers at the assembly plant and the regional stamping plant. |
| During 2027 | Retooling under the $1.25 billion program. GM expects some employees back before the year ends. |
| Second half of 2028 | Next-generation CT5 production begins (2029 model year). |

The decision points sit between the rows. **Before December 2026**, workers choose between transfer offers and layoff with benefits; suppliers decide how much CT5 work to run ahead and bank. **In early 2027**, laid-off workers decide whether to wait for recall, take training, or leave the industry — a decision that depends on how credible the 2028 restart date looks. **Through 2027**, mid-Michigan suppliers decide whether to hold their own skilled people through the hole or cut them and try to rehire later. **In late 2027**, the first recalls for retooling and launch work are the earliest hard evidence that the restart is on schedule. Every one of those decisions is being made now, on the information available now.

## What 74% means over 18 months

As a household budget over a year and a half, 74% is a specific amount of missing money. The arithmetic below is **illustrative** — it uses a round wage to show the shape of the problem, not any individual worker's actual pay or benefit:

- Take a production worker earning **$35 an hour**, 40 hours a week: **$1,400 a week** gross.
- At 74%, income support is about **$1,036 a week** — a shortfall of roughly **$364 a week**, or about **$1,580 a month**.
- If that worker were out for a full year, the forgone wages total about **$18,900**; across the full 18 months, about **$28,400**.

Three qualifications, all of which cut in different directions. First, GM does not expect all 350 workers to be out for the entire changeover — transfers and staggered recalls will shorten the gap for many. Second, supplemental benefits are not unlimited; eligibility and duration rules in the contract will bind for anyone out the longest. Third, the worker who transfers to another GM plant keeps full pay but may take on a commute measured in hours. The honest summary is that the 74% figure converts a layoff from an emergency into a slow squeeze — survivable for a household with savings, corrosive for one without, and worse the longer the gap runs. That last clause is the one to watch.

## Michigan has run this experiment before — at Orion

Forty miles east, GM's Orion Assembly plant is the comparison case, and it is not reassuring. Orion built the Chevrolet Bolt EV until production ended in **December 2023**. The plant was to be retooled — a $4 billion program — to build electric Silverado and Sierra pickups starting in 2024. The start slipped to 2025, then to 2026. Then, in **June 2025**, GM changed the product entirely: Orion will build gasoline-powered Silverado, Sierra, and Cadillac Escalade trucks and SUVs instead, with production now expected to start in **2027** ([Reuters via TradingView](https://www.tradingview.com/news/reuters.com,2025:newsml_L6N3TC0RR:0-gm-outlines-4-billion-plan-to-expand-production-of-gas-trucks-and-suvs/); [GM Authority](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2026/05/heres-when-the-gm-orion-plant-will-start-building-pickups-and-suvs/)). A gap planned at roughly a year ran past three, and the vehicle waiting at the end of it was not the vehicle promised at the start.

The lesson is not that Lansing's gap will stretch — Lansing's is a model changeover for a confirmed car, not a technology bet. The lesson is about *where slippage comes from*. Orion's timeline did not fail because the retooling was hard. It failed because the product plan underneath it kept moving. Lansing's plan has already moved once: the $1.25 billion was an EV conversion until it became a gasoline sedan program. The 2028 date is credible exactly as long as the CT5 program stays settled — and reports of other unconfirmed products for the plant (a next-generation Camaro, a Buick sedan) are, for now, speculation GM has not endorsed.

## The options nobody is pricing

Most coverage treats the gap as dead time. It does not have to be, and three uses of it are going unexamined:

**Train into the new plant, not just back to the old one.** The retooled Lansing Grand River will run updated processes for a new vehicle generation. The layoff period is the only long, uninterrupted training window the workforce will ever get. Whether GM, the UAW, and the state's workforce system use it to bring workers back *more* skilled — certified on the new line's equipment and quality systems before launch, rather than retrained on the job after it — is a choice being made by default right now.

**Keep the stamping plant warm.** Lansing Regional Stamping presses body panels. A stamping plant that goes fully cold for 18 months loses die-setting and press expertise that is slow to rebuild; one that stamps for other GM programs keeps its people and its tolerances. GM has not said which future the stamping plant gets. It is one of the most consequential unannounced decisions in the whole program.

**Plan for the supplier base, not just the plant.** The 350 WARN-counted jobs are GM's. The hole also runs through every mid-Michigan supplier tooled for CT5 volumes. Suppliers that cut skilled staff in 2027 will bid on 2028 launch work with whoever they can hire in 2028. A gap that "goes fine" for GM and badly for its suppliers is a launch problem arriving on schedule.

## What would have to be true

We will judge this gap the way we judge any plan: by checkable conditions, stated in advance.

**The gap is going well if:** GM holds the second-half-2028 restart and says so consistently; the first recalls land in 2027 as GM has indicated; training seats for laid-off workers exist, are tied to the retooled plant's processes, and are filled; the stamping plant is visibly working, for Lansing or for other GM programs; and the CT5 remains the plant's confirmed product with no further program reversals.

**The gap is going badly if:** the restart estimate starts drifting right (Orion's pattern was one quiet slip at a time); "temporary" layoffs are extended or converted; the product at the end of the gap changes again; or the supplier base around Lansing visibly thins — closures, consolidations, skilled-trades postings that go unfilled at launch suppliers.

None of those signals requires inside information. All of them are observable from public filings, GM statements, and local reporting between now and 2028. We will be watching them, and we will report what they show either way. A plant that stops for 18 months is not a crisis. A plant that stops for 18 months *without a plan for the interval* becomes one slowly, and then all at once at launch. Lansing has the dates. What it does with them is the story.
