Research at Axiovex

Big questions, tested where everyone can check.

Alongside our client work, Axiovex Systems runs a small, open research program on the biggest questions we know how to ask: What is time? What is gravity? How did everything start — and why are some questions so hard to answer at all? This page says what we have learned so far, in plain language, and how we go about learning it.

Program record as of October 13, 2026 The registered studies ↗·The framing paper and the ledger ↗·Research at BitConcepts ↗

What stands

  • Seven of the program's tested claims have been confirmed outright — including the boundary counting law, which held at every scale we measured, and the result that a quantum record's texture does not depend on how strongly its dynamics mixes.
  • The framework's core results stand as proved, with their premises stated openly — including the proof, checked by computer, that gravity's extra rule does not follow from the framework's basic assumptions.
  • The method itself: every claim on this page was registered publicly before its test ran, and every verdict — for us or against us — is published at the same size.

What does not stand

  • Most claims we have tested have failed their tests. Of the studies closed so far, the rejected and no-effect outcomes outnumber the confirmed ones — that is the record, and this page does not soften it.
  • Our first strong idea about perception — that how reportable an experience is tracks how redundantly its record is spread across the brain — failed in its pooled form (Study 018) and is retired in that form.
  • Nothing on this page is carried as accepted. In the program's own five stages, the claims here stand at checked and explained; acceptance requires independent researchers to take them up, and that has not happened yet.

Program one — Projected constraints

Most of science describes the world as stuff — particles, fields, forces — and then asks what the stuff does. This program starts one step earlier. It describes a system by the limits on it: the rules about what can happen and what cannot. Then it asks a simple question with surprising reach — if all we ever get to see is what those limits allow through, what does the world have to look like? Internally, the program is called RCPH. Everything it claims is registered publicly before the test runs, and the result is published either way — including when the answer is "we were wrong."

What we have learned so far

Time is a pattern, not a metronome. We built systems with no clock in them at all — no time variable anywhere in the description — and the pattern we call time showed up anyway, in the way the system's records are organized. Time, in this picture, is something the records do, not a background tick everything dances to. It behaves the way a summary behaves: a compressed way of presenting a structure that does not itself unfold. One more finding, seen again and again in our tests: when these systems change, they tend to flip, not slide — a state holds, holds, holds, and then switches, rather than melting gradually from one form into the next.

Gravity may be counting. Take any region and draw its boundary — a fence. Now count the connections that cross the fence. The count grows with the size of the fence, not with the amount of stuff inside it. That is exactly how gravity behaves in the real world: its strength at a boundary depends on area, not volume, and nobody has ever fully explained why. In our framework the area pattern falls out of pure counting. Here is the honest catch, and we state it as plainly as the result: the counting argument needs one extra rule — a limit on how much a boundary can carry. We tried to derive that rule from the framework's basic assumptions, and we could not. So we did the next best thing: we proved, with the proof checked by computer, that the rule genuinely does not follow from those assumptions. It is not a hidden step in our math. It is an adopted premise, listed openly, and anyone who wants to challenge the gravity result knows exactly which premise to aim at.

Everything may have started by un-squishing. Our models of the early universe start from a small, compressed state. We found that a single decompression — one un-squishing, unfolding into the structured state we observe — is enough. The universe does not need to bounce or cycle forever to produce what we see. One detail turned out to matter enormously: the compressed starting state has to be an ordinary one, a typical example of its kind. Pick a special, hand-tuned starting state and the result falls apart. Ordinariness is load-bearing.

Seeing is a report — and reports can be tested. Part of this program studies perception: what happens in the brain when a person sees something faintly, at the edge of awareness, and says "I saw it" — or doesn't. That report side of seeing is testable, and we test it in registered studies on public brain-imaging data. Our first strong idea about it failed its test, publicly, and we retired it. But there is a second side — what seeing feels like from the inside — and our work marks a hard boundary there: no record, of any kind, in any experiment, can carry the feeling itself, because records are exactly the things that get reported, stored, and read back. That is not an excuse for not studying it. It is a result about what kind of question it is.

Why some questions stay stuck. After dozens of tests, the failures fell into three piles, and the piles turned out to be more interesting than most of the successes. A question can stay stuck because the thing it asks about is already maxed out — like asking whether a full glass can get fuller. It can stay stuck because the answer isn't in the record — the window we are allowed to look through was never given that page. Or it can stay stuck because the signal is too faint to measure — written down, but below the floor of what any instrument can read. Gravity's extra rule, the feeling side of seeing, the faint perception signals — each of our open questions now has a named wall it is facing. Knowing which wall you are up against is not the same as getting through it, but it beats not knowing.

The framework — projected constraints

"Projected constraints" is the name of the framework behind Program one. It has five working ideas. None of them needs mathematics to state, and every one of them is doing real work in the results above.

1. Start with limits, not stuff. A constraint is a rule about what cannot happen — a wall, a budget, a conservation law. Describe a system by its constraints and you have described everything that could ever be observed about it, without committing to what it is "made of" underneath. When the usual description of a thing keeps failing, it is often because the thing was never the right level to describe. The limits were.

2. Every view comes through an interface. Nobody observes a system directly. We observe it through a boundary — an instrument, a sense organ, a dataset — and the boundary decides what can be asked and what can be answered. The framework calls that boundary an interface, and it treats the choice of interface as part of the description, never as a detail. Change the interface and the same underlying system presents a different visible world. A good deal of confusion, in science and elsewhere, is two people arguing about two different interfaces as if they were arguing about the world.

3. Records are all we ever get. A record is anything that persists and can be read back: a measurement, a photograph, a memory, a data file, a fossil. Every fact anyone has ever checked arrived as a record. So the framework studies records directly — what a record can contain, what it cannot contain no matter how it is made, and what follows from that. The boundary around the feeling side of seeing, above, is one such result: it is a theorem about records before it is a claim about minds.

4. Counting is unforgiving — and that is its power. Many of the framework's results come from counting: how many connections cross a boundary, how many arrangements satisfy a set of constraints, how many distinct things an interface can report. Counting does not care what we hope is true. It is how the area pattern behind gravity appears, and it is also how several of our own favorite ideas died — the count said no, in public, and that was the end of them.

5. The method: register, run, publish — either way. Every claim in this program is handled the same way. First, state the claim and the exact test that could kill it, and register both publicly, before any computation runs. Second, run exactly what was registered — no quiet adjustments afterward. Third, publish the verdict whichever way it lands; a falsified claim is listed with the same prominence as a confirmed one. Fourth, have the work audited by reviewers who did not do it. And underneath all of it, the formal mathematics is kept in a form a computer can check, so an argument cannot quietly drift away from its own assumptions. We are loyal to the test, not to the idea. An idea that fails is retired, in public, and the program moves on to a better one.

What the framework has produced

A counting law for how much information fits inside a boundary holds exactly in the model — Study 024 on the board is its registered test. The extra condition it needs from nature is still open — and we proved the current axioms cannot settle it either way.

Two operations that sound alike — averaging away fine detail, and treating symmetric states as identical — are provably not the same operation. One of them is licensed by the axioms; the other is not.

For the standard recovery test, the derived failure points matched the computations across the whole Petz line (Studies 028–032) — and one knob, disorder in the reference, made violations rarer as it grew: a noise that repairs instead of deepening.

The five stages

Every claim in the program passes through the same five stages, and a claim is only ever as settled as its stage.

1CreatedThe claim is stated, with the test that could kill it. 2CheckedThe registered test runs and the verdict is taken. 3ExplainedThe reasoning is written out so a named reader can follow and challenge it. 4AcceptedIndependent researchers take the claim up and it survives their hands. 5DigestedThe claim and its evidence are part of how the field works; nothing about it needs us anymore.

The claims on this page stand at checked and explained — the two marked stages. None is carried as accepted or digested.

Two lanes

Checking runs at machine speed — a test can be re-run in an afternoon. Acceptance moves at human pace — it takes other people, reading and judging, over months. The two are tracked separately, and the first is never displayed as the second.

Corrections are appended, never rewritten

A changed assessment is a new, dated record laid beside the old one. Original records and issued verdicts are never rewritten.

October 13, 2026

No measurable stand-in for the feeling of seeing survives our own rules

Basis: the phenomenal-observable study, program record of 2026-10-13.

Read the full article ↓

The question. The program studies what seeing reports — that side is testable. What seeing feels like is fenced off, unless something measurable could stand in for the feeling without simply being the report renamed. This study asked what such a stand-in would have to look like for the program to adopt one, and scored the serious candidates against the answer.

The requirements. Five, each forced by a rule the program already runs on: a stated measuring procedure anyone can carry out; the quantity must not be report renamed; the link between the quantity and experience (the bridge) must be checkable against evidence that does not come from the quantity itself; the quantity must discriminate what experience is like, not only whether it is present; and it must fit inside the framework's scope.

What the candidates did. The level of consciousness — whether experience is present, and roughly how much — is genuinely measurable. The strongest measure (perturb the brain, and score how complex the echo is) passes the procedure requirement and is plainly not report renamed. But it reaches level and presence only. And every candidate failed the same requirement — the bridge. A bridge can only be checked against an anchor, and the only anchor anyone has is report: what people say they experienced. Where report exists, the stand-in adds nothing the program does not already test directly. Where report is absent — the unresponsive patient, the no-report trial, the non-human system, exactly the cases a stand-in would earn its keep — no check is possible, so no kill condition exists, and the claim cannot be sorted.

The verdict. No candidate survives. One leading candidate (a quantity computed from a system's causal structure) failed the procedure requirement at its own authors' statement. Nothing measurable anywhere in the survey touches phenomenal character — what experience is like, as opposed to whether it is there. This is a verdict about what can be adopted as a measurement — not a claim that experience is unreal, or that it is the same thing as a report.

What would change it. The study names its exits: a complexity measure taken while a patient cannot report, registered in advance to predict what the patient later reports on recovery, done to standard across cohorts; a structural prediction made away from the anchor; independent measures converging without recovery cases. Until one of those exists, the fence stands — by result, not by preference.

October 13, 2026

The consciousness line reopens

Basis: the consciousness-line reactivation report, 2026-10-13.

Read the full article ↓

The line's audit found a consequential bookkeeping error. Study 018 rejected the pooled form of the record-redundancy idea — but the within-person contrast form was never the thing that study's verdict bore on, and the contrast measurement inside it was positive in all 22 subjects. The line had been carried as if the idea had failed wholesale. It had not: the contrast survives, unjudged, at even odds.

Study 027's failure was diagnosed properly. Its test asked whether the orientation of a faint stimulus could be read from scalp recordings — and orientation cannot be read from scalp recordings even when the stimulus is plainly visible. The arena failed before the claim could: only 1 of 40 recordings was usable at the standard fixed in advance.

The line therefore reopens with readability as its first problem. A pilot on a suitable dataset must show the perception signal can be read at all — a median per-subject reading accuracy of at least 0.60, across at least 30 subjects — before any claim test may be frozen. The dataset deposit for the pilot is authorized; the pilot measures readability only and takes no claim statistics.

Two further candidate forms — a no-report variant and a metacognition variant of the surviving claim — are staged for formulation. And the fence question was put and answered the same day: phenomenology stays out of scope, and the companion study (above) shows the program's own rules are what keep it there.

October 12, 2026

The whole history, re-examined

Basis: the framework realignment and full-history audit, 2026-10-12.

Read the full article ↓

The program adopted a stricter discipline for what counts as settled. A claim passes through five stages — created, checked, explained, accepted, digested — and creation plus checking alone never settles it.

Every headline result was broken into its parts: is the statement faithful? is the evidence verified? is the claim genuinely open? is the attribution clean? does it matter? Each part scored on its own, with its own falsifier. Digestion — explanation a named reader can follow and challenge — became first-class tracked work, and a result that is verified but unexplained is now flagged as the risk it is.

Checking and acceptance are tracked as two lanes that never merge on paper: machine-speed verification, and human-paced acceptance. Easily graded counts never stand in for understanding, and every verdict states what remains not understood.

The discipline was then applied backwards, to the whole history: every closed study re-examined, corrections made only as new dated records beside the old — frozen records and issued verdicts were never altered, and what cannot be fixed is labelled a historical limitation in the open. Two results of that audit are on this page: the consciousness contrast was found unjudged rather than failed, and the verdict tallies that once appeared here were retired.

Program two — The epistemic ledger

The second program is about evidence itself. In most fields, a claim's credibility lives in people's heads and in the reputations of the people repeating it. The epistemic ledger gives a claim a public, revisable grade instead: a score that goes up when evidence survives testing and down when it fails, with the reasons for every change written next to it, in the open, where anyone can argue with them. A grade is never a verdict from authority — it is a running summary of what the evidence has done so far, and it is allowed to move.

The framework behind the ledger — why grades should move the way they do, and what keeps the bookkeeping honest — is published as a framing paper, and the ledger itself is maintained in a public repository (link below). The same grading discipline runs underneath Program one: it is how we keep score on our own claims, and it is why every result above is stated with its mechanism and its reasons attached, never as a bare count.

Framing paper published; ledger public and maintained; the method is in active use across both programs.
