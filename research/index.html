Research at Axiovex

Big questions, tested where everyone can check.

Alongside our client work, Axiovex Systems runs a small, open research program on the biggest questions we know how to ask: What is time? What is gravity? How did everything start — and why are some questions so hard to answer at all? This page says what we have learned so far, in plain language, and how we go about learning it.

What stands

  • Nine of the program's tested claims have been confirmed outright — including the boundary counting law, which held at every scale we measured in one space dimension and now also in two; the result that a quantum record's texture does not depend on how strongly its dynamics mixes; and what holds a record's starting point in place: not the pair-flipping couplings themselves, nor the pattern they follow, but the filled channel they occupy — a slot other couplings can substitute into.
  • The framework's core results stand as proved, with their premises stated openly — including the proof, checked by computer, that gravity's extra rule does not follow from the framework's basic assumptions.
  • The registered-study method: the studies on the public register state their claims and tests in advance, and publish their verdicts either way. The separate finite-model report below states its own protocol and publication history.

What does not stand

  • Most claims we have tested have failed their tests. Of the studies closed so far, the rejected and no-effect outcomes outnumber the confirmed ones — that is the record, and this page does not soften it.
  • Our first strong idea about perception — that how reportable an experience is tracks how redundantly its record is spread across the brain — failed in its pooled form (Study 018) and is retired in that form.
  • Nothing on this page is carried as accepted. In the program's own five stages, the claims here stand at checked and explained; acceptance requires independent researchers to take them up, and that has not happened yet. The invitation is now formally out — see "Replication, invited," below.

Program one — Projected constraints

Most of science describes the world as stuff — particles, fields, forces — and then asks what the stuff does. This program starts one step earlier. It describes a system by the limits on it: the rules about what can happen and what cannot. Then it asks a simple question with surprising reach — if all we ever get to see is what those limits allow through, what does the world have to look like? Internally, the program is called RCPH. Every claim is written down with the test that could kill it, frozen, and registered publicly before the test runs — and the result is published either way, including when the answer is "we were wrong." (One study's registration paperwork needed repair after the fact; the note under 'The studies' below tells that story in full.) The separate finite-model report in Latest research records a protocol freeze before its first computation and a later public release; it is not an additional public registration in that series.

What we have learned so far

Time is a pattern, not a metronome. We built systems with no clock in them at all — no time variable anywhere in the description — and the pattern we call time showed up anyway, in the way the system's records are organized. Time, in this picture, is something the records do, not a background tick everything dances to. It behaves the way a summary behaves: a compressed way of presenting a structure that does not itself unfold. One more finding, seen again and again in our tests: when these systems change, they tend to flip, not slide — a state holds, holds, holds, and then switches, rather than melting gradually from one form into the next.

Gravity may be counting. Take any region and cut it at its widest slice. Now count the elements that straddle the cut — the last layer inside it and the first layer outside. That count grows with the size of the slice, not with the amount of stuff inside the region. A pattern like that shows up in real physics in a famous place: the most a region of space can hold — measured as information, or disorder — grows with the area of its boundary, not with its volume. Black holes are where physics was first forced to face this, and nobody has ever fully explained why nature keeps its books by area. Counts of this kind were first studied in the causal-set literature in 2015, which established the growth law and its exact constants for surfaces without end. What this program added is the same count for a finite region — whose slice has an edge, and the edge changes the count by an amount that can be computed exactly — and registered tests of those exact numbers, on the board below. Here is the honest catch, and we state it as plainly as the result: the counting argument needs one extra rule — a limit on how much a boundary can carry. We tried to derive that rule from the framework's basic assumptions, and we could not. So we did the next best thing: we proved, with the proof checked by computer, that the rule genuinely does not follow from those assumptions. It is not a hidden step in our math. It is an adopted premise, listed openly, and anyone who wants to challenge the gravity result knows exactly which premise to aim at.

Everything may have started by un-squishing. Our models of the early universe start from a small, compressed state. We found that a single decompression — one un-squishing, unfolding into the structured state we observe — is enough. The universe does not need to bounce or cycle forever to produce what we see. One detail turned out to matter enormously: the compressed starting state has to be an ordinary one, a typical example of its kind. Pick a special, hand-tuned starting state and the result falls apart. Ordinariness is load-bearing.

Seeing is a report — and reports can be tested. Part of this program studies perception: what happens in the brain when a person sees something faintly, at the edge of awareness, and says "I saw it" — or doesn't. That report side of seeing is testable, and we test it in registered studies on public brain-imaging data. Our first strong idea about it failed its test, publicly, and we retired it. But there is a second side — what seeing feels like from the inside. We surveyed the serious scientific candidates for measuring that side directly, and tested each against a strict standard: a real measurement procedure, not a renaming of what people report, with a link to experience that could in principle fail its check. None met the standard. The strongest candidate — a score built from how the brain answers a magnetic pulse — measures whether experience is present, and roughly how rich it is, but not what it is like. Every route we found from a measurement to the feeling itself still passes through what the person reports; and where reports are absent, the link cannot be checked at all. That is a result about the present state of the question — no candidate has met the standard yet — not a proof that none ever could.

Why some questions stay stuck. After dozens of tests, the failures fell into three piles, and the piles turned out to be more interesting than most of the successes. A question can stay stuck because the thing it asks about is already maxed out — like asking whether a full glass can get fuller. It can stay stuck because the answer isn't in the record — the window we are allowed to look through was never given that page. Or it can stay stuck because the signal is too faint to measure — written down, but below the floor of what any instrument can read. Gravity's extra rule, the feeling side of seeing, the faint perception signals — each of our open questions now has a named wall it is facing. Knowing which wall you are up against is not the same as getting through it, but it beats not knowing.

The framework — projected constraints

"Projected constraints" is the name of the framework behind Program one. It has five working ideas. None of them needs mathematics to state, and every one of them is doing real work in the results above.

1. Start with limits, not stuff. A constraint is a rule about what cannot happen — a wall, a budget, a conservation law. Describe a system by its constraints and you have described everything that could ever be observed about it, without committing to what it is "made of" underneath. When the usual description of a thing keeps failing, it is often because the thing was never the right level to describe. The limits were.

2. Every view comes through an interface. Nobody observes a system directly. We observe it through a boundary — an instrument, a sense organ, a dataset — and the boundary decides what can be asked and what can be answered. The framework calls that boundary an interface, and it treats the choice of interface as part of the description, never as a detail. Change the interface and the same underlying system presents a different visible world. A good deal of confusion, in science and elsewhere, is two people arguing about two different interfaces as if they were arguing about the world.

3. Records are all we ever get. A record is anything that persists and can be read back: a measurement, a photograph, a memory, a data file, a fossil. Every fact anyone has ever checked arrived as a record. So the framework studies records directly — what a record can contain, what it cannot contain no matter how it is made, and what follows from that. The boundary around the feeling side of seeing, above, is one such result: it is a finding about what records can carry, stated before any claim about minds.

4. Counting is unforgiving — and that is its power. Many of the framework's results come from counting: how many connections cross a boundary, how many arrangements satisfy a set of constraints, how many distinct things an interface can report. Counting does not care what we hope is true. It is how the area pattern behind gravity appears, and it is also how several of our own favorite ideas died — the count said no, in public, and that was the end of them.

5. The method: register, run, publish — either way. The public registered-study series follows this method. First, state the claim and the exact test that could kill it, and register both publicly, before any computation runs. Second, run exactly what was registered — no quiet adjustments afterward. Third, publish the verdict whichever way it lands; a falsified claim is listed with the same prominence as a confirmed one. Fourth, have the work audited by reviewers who did not do it. And underneath all of it, the formal mathematics is kept in a form a computer can check, so an argument cannot quietly drift away from its own assumptions. We are loyal to the test, not to the idea. An idea that fails is retired, in public, and the program moves on to a better one.

What the framework has produced

A counting law for how much information fits inside a boundary holds exactly in the model in one space dimension and in two — Studies 024 and 038 on the board are its registered tests. The extra condition it needs from nature is still open — and we proved the current axioms cannot settle it either way.

Two operations that sound alike — averaging away fine detail, and treating symmetric states as identical — are provably not the same operation. One of them is licensed by the axioms; the other is not.

For the standard recovery test, the derived failure points matched the computations across the whole Petz line (Studies 028–032) — and one knob, disorder in the reference, made violations rarer as it grew: a noise that repairs instead of deepening.

The five stages

Every claim in the program passes through the same five stages, and a claim is only ever as settled as its stage.

1CreatedThe claim is stated, with the test that could kill it. 2CheckedThe registered test runs and the verdict is taken. 3ExplainedThe reasoning is written out so a named reader can follow and challenge it. 4AcceptedIndependent researchers take the claim up and it survives their hands. 5DigestedThe claim and its evidence are part of how the field works; nothing about it needs us anymore.

The claims on this page stand at checked and explained — the two marked stages. None is carried as accepted or digested.

Two lanes

Checking runs at machine speed — a test can be re-run in an afternoon. Acceptance moves at human pace — it takes other people, reading and judging, over months. The two are tracked separately, and the first is never displayed as the second.

Replication, invited

Checking our own work is only half of the method; the other half is other people checking it. In October 2026 we publicly invited independent researchers to re-run two sets of this program's results. No permission is needed and no contact with us is required — each invitation points at a public record that carries the registration, the frozen protocol, the code, and the complete results:

  • The recovery family. Five studies of a standard quantum recovery method (the Petz map). Each one derived, before any computation, a specific number for where the method's recovery guarantee holds and where it fails, and then tested that number: a dephasing onset, a recovery-deficit curve, a restoration threshold, a gap curve, and a disorder-ensemble profile. Registrations: osf.io/b8skx · osf.io/yrcnx · osf.io/jrhua · osf.io/r5y2g · osf.io/q8tmg.
  • The boundary counting law. How the separator between a region and its surroundings grows with the region's size, in sprinkled causal sets — the counting result behind the gravity discussion above. Registration: osf.io/67cr9.

The invitations went to the communities that work on these questions. The counting-law notice was submitted to the Hyperspace bulletin, the news roundup for the gravity and causal-set community. The recovery invitation has been posted to Quantiki, the quantum-information community's board, and to a quantum software engineering mailing list run through the ACM. One more list we wrote to turned out to be defunct: the message bounced, undelivered. We record that here rather than count it as an invitation sent.

The state of play is: invited, unanswered. That phrase has a strict meaning on this page. A result counts as taken up only when a named researcher outside this program re-runs it and publishes the outcome — agreeing with us or not. Silence is not acceptance; neither are downloads or citations. If a re-run happens, its outcome will be linked from the study's own record and noted here, whichever way it lands.

October 22, 2026 The boundary counting law held in two space dimensions, with the predicted finite-size number. Basis: Study 038's verdict and digestion records, program records of 2026-10-22. The study and its timing pilot were registered in advance; the pilot's samples were excluded from the study. Read the full article ↓

The question. Take a region of spacetime shaped like a diamond — two light cones placed tip to tip — and scatter points through it at random, keeping only the causal order: which points can influence which. Cut the region at its waist, the widest slice, which the diamond's own shape selects. The separator is the layer of points straddling that cut: just below it, the points with nothing above them in the lower half; just above it, the points with nothing below them in the upper half. An earlier study in this program tested this layer's count in a model with one space dimension, and it followed a boundary law: it grows with the boundary's size, not with the amount of space inside. (The law itself, for counts of this kind at a spacelike surface, was first established in the causal-set literature in 2015; this program's studies test its exact finite-size form in finite regions.) This study asked whether the law also holds with two space dimensions — and whether it holds with the exact finite-size number a derivation produced before any counting.

Why the finite-size number was the real test. At endlessly large sizes, the derivation says the count per unit of waist area approaches a fixed constant, 1.7587. No feasible computation reaches that limit. At the sizes that can be run, the waist's rim still matters: points near the rim are more likely to sit in the layer, and that correction pulls the measured ratio below the limit — by about 10% at the smallest size used here and by about 5% at the largest. A test against the limiting constant alone would therefore have blurred the question. Before the run, the derivation supplied an exact prediction for each size, rim correction included, and the study froze two separate judgments: the growth law would be judged by its exponent, and the coefficient would be judged against the exact finite-size prediction at the largest size.

What happened. Every example completed and counted: 72 of 72, across four sizes — 24 at 2,000 points, 20 at 4,000, 16 at 8,000, and 12 at 16,000. The measured ratios followed the derivation's finite-size path at every size: 1.5832 against 1.5875 predicted at the smallest size; 1.6335 against 1.6217; 1.6525 against 1.6493; and, at the largest size, 1.6603 against 1.6715. None differed by more than 0.8%. The fitted growth exponent was 0.6890, inside the registered support band around two-thirds; the step-by-step exponents between sizes descended toward two-thirds, as the finite-size structure predicts. The layer's two sides contributed almost equally throughout. The checks on the same examples also passed: links crossing the waist grew with exponent 1.3520, close to the derived four-thirds; all causal relations grew with exponent 2.0019, the square law expected for a bulk count; and the normalized crossing count at the largest size landed within 0.06% of a separately computed target. Before the campaign, a brute-force counter agreed exactly with the production counter on 16 test examples, and a 40-example anchor run at the smallest campaign size reproduced the predicted ratio.

The outcome. Both registered tracks are confirmed. The separator count follows the area growth law in this two-space-dimension model, and it does so with the derivation's exact finite-size coefficient — not merely with the right kind of growth. That distinction is the heart of the result: the rim correction was large enough that a derivation with the limiting constant right but the rim term wrong would have missed visibly, especially at the smallest size. It did not.

What this does not show. This is a counting law in a model, not a demonstration about physical gravity. Reading it as gravity would require an extra premise — that this counted layer is the boundary meant by the separate formal treatment — and this study did not test that premise. It remains openly listed, exactly as before. The count is not identified with entropy, and the study claims no gravitational coefficient. It also covers only two space dimensions and one time dimension, and only the waist slice: whether a physically tilted slice of equal area through the same diamond would carry an equal expected count was not measured and remains an open question.

A stated verification limit. The derivation was re-derived inside the program by a second analytic route, with the two routes agreeing exactly, and its finite-size numbers were checked by high-precision numerical calculation. A separate blind computer engine was also asked to reproduce the derivation, but it failed its own known-answer control before voting, so its answers counted for nothing. No independent outside derivation has yet reproduced the algebra. The measurements agree with the derivation at four sizes; that is evidence for it, but it is not the same thing as an independent derivation.

What remains open. The limiting constant is approached along the predicted path, never reached at a runnable size. One technical gap named when the study was registered has since been closed: the derivation predicts a ratio of averages, while the judged measurement averages each example's ratio, and that difference has now been computed exactly, in a check made after the verdict and filed on the program's record. At the largest size it moves the expected ratio by six ten-thousandths of one percent — far inside the registered band — so it touches neither the verdict nor any number above. Larger sizes could still depart from the predicted curve, and the tilted-slice question could still reframe what the law is a law of. No successor study is registered, and none is promised here.

The second readability pilot, closed.

The question. The same question as the first pilot, asked of a second dataset. Before the surviving perception claim — that within a person, reportability tracks how redundantly an experience's record is spread — can be tested at all, some dataset must let the signal be read: what a faint stimulus was, from scalp recordings, person by person, at a bar fixed in advance. This pilot tested a public deposit of brain recordings from 35 people in a near-threshold detection experiment in Madrid — one chosen because every trial in it also records the person's own report of seeing, which is what a later claim test would need. The pilot computed readability only, and no claim statistics of any kind.

What happened. All 35 people in the deposit were resolved. In four, the recording could not be scored at all by the method frozen in advance — three files carry a doubled set of recording channels and one was recorded at a different sampling rate than the rest; the deposit's own listings disagree with its files for those people — and they count as not clearing, as the gate's definition, fixed before the run, requires. Of the 31 who could be scored, 20 cleared the frozen floor; the gate required at least 30 of the 35. The typical clear reading was 0.5935 against the required 0.60, on a scale where 0.50 is chance. The reading itself is genuine: across all 31 scored people the typical reading was 0.5605, and readings under shuffled labels sat at chance (0.4973) — the method is not inventing signal. A simpler control reading from the same recordings — whether anything had appeared at all — came in at a typical 0.5857, comparable to the main reading, as expected when the stimulus is physically present on every trial; it neither rescues nor undermines the gate, which was set on the main reading alone.

The outcome. The pilot failed its gate, on both counts — the count of clear readers and the typical clear reading each fell short. Judged either way the record allows, the answer is the same: the gate's own reading fails, and the typical reading across everyone scored is also below the bar. That is a finding about this dataset and this reading task — what a faint stimulus was is not readable strongly enough, in enough people, in this deposit. It is not a finding about perception, and it is not a verdict on the claim, which was never computed here and stands unjudged. With this result, three public datasets have now been examined as arenas for the claim — in the first, the content could not be read even for clearly visible stimuli; in the second and third, the gate failed — and no audited public arena currently works for it.

What happens next. This pilot's work is done: it does not search for a further dataset, and nothing on this line is currently running. The claim stays unjudged, behind the same rule as before — no claim test is frozen until an arena passes the readability gate. The pilot's record leaves one question open, and names it as the program's to decide, not the pilot's: the deposit's two file-format quirks are arguably repairable by redesigning the method for them, which would raise the number of people who could be scored in a future pilot. No decision has been taken, and none is promised here; if one is, it will be reported here.

The onset question, answered.

The question. What holds a quantum record's starting point — its onset, the fragment size at which the record first stands complete — at its usual depth? An earlier finding said that removing one family of couplings, the ones that flip pairs of spins together, moves the onset earlier. Three explanations were registered in advance: the specific family does the holding; the family's even-flip pattern does it; or the channel the family fills — the links between pairs of spins — does it, simply by being filled. Substitute couplings, at the same count and the same total strength, were the test built to separate them. The first asking of this question closed unjudged, when its largest examples could not be completed; this entry's study was the second asking, on fresh examples.

What happened. Every one of the eighteen examples finished, the largest size included. Removing the pair-flipping family moved the onset earlier in 17 of 18 — by one step at the smaller sizes, by two at the largest, by three in a single case; the advance grows with size. Removing a different family did essentially nothing. And both substitutes held the onset exactly, in every example: one flipping four spins at once, keeping the even pattern; one flipping three, breaking it.

The outcome. The claim is confirmed: what holds the onset is the filled channel, not the couplings that fill it and not the pattern they follow — the one registered explanation, of the three, that predicted this pattern. A registered secondary check repeated the earlier separation on this complete sample: the interventions move where the record starts without thinning how strongly it stands. Where a record starts, and how tall it stands, come apart.

What remains unexplained. Why a filled channel sets the depth at all. Whether "capacity" is the count of couplings, their total strength, or the two together — the substitutes matched both at once, by design. And whether the deepening continues past the largest size run. The study's record names the tests that would sharpen or break the answer; none is registered yet.

The readability pilot, closed.

The question. Before the surviving perception claim — that within a person, reportability tracks how redundantly an experience's record is spread — can be tested at all, some dataset must let the signal be read: where a faint stimulus appeared, from scalp recordings, person by person, at a bar fixed in advance. This pilot tested one public deposit against that bar, and nothing else — it computed readability only, and no claim statistics of any kind.

What happened. All 56 people in the deposit were run; in three, the deposit held too few usable trials of the needed kind for a location reading to be computed at all. The location reading cleared the frozen floor in 15 people — the gate required at least 30 — and the typical clear reading was 0.587 against the required 0.60, on a scale where 0.50 is chance. A control reading from the same recordings, whether anything had appeared at all, came in at a typical 0.589, with shuffled controls at chance: the recordings and the reading method do carry signal.

The outcome. The pilot failed its gate, on both counts. That is a finding about this dataset and this reading task — the location signal is not readable strongly enough, in enough people, in this deposit. It is not a finding about perception, and it is not a verdict on the claim, which was never computed here and stands unjudged.

What happens next. A second pilot is running, on a different dataset — one that records the person's own report of seeing on every trial. If it passes the same kind of gate, the claim test can be frozen. If it fails, that failure will be published here the same way.

October 20, 2026 The onset study closed unjudged, and its question was asked again the same day. Basis: the study's verdict and digestion records and the successor's registration, program records of 2026-10-20. Read the full article ↓

The question. When a redundant record of a quantum system's state first appears, it appears at a particular fragment size — its starting point, or onset. An earlier study found that removing one family of couplings — the ones that flip pairs of spins together — moved the onset one step earlier, every time, while removing other families never did. This study asked, on fresh examples, whether that family is what holds the onset in place, with substitute families ready to separate three rival explanations of how it might do so.

What happened. On every example that finished, the pattern repeated: the onset moved earlier in 11 of 12 when the pair-flipping family was removed, and under no other removal at all. But the six largest examples never finished. Each ran for hours and was then killed by the computing environment, six times out of six. Why they were killed is not established; the pattern fits the machine running out of memory, and the record labels that reading unverified rather than proven.

The outcome. Under the rules registered in advance, a sample missing its entire largest size cannot judge the claim. The study closed unjudged — not confirmed, not refuted. One secondary check did add a clean fact: removing the family moves where the record starts without weakening how strongly it stands afterward. Where a record starts, and how tall it stands, come apart.

What happens next. The question was registered again on October 20, 2026, on fresh examples, with the execution rebuilt so the largest examples can finish — and it is now running. A completed largest size, showing the pattern or its absence, is what will judge the claim.

October 19, 2026 What a record can tell us — and what it leaves out Basis: the recovered discriminating-studies report and a separate runtime validation update, October 19, 2026. Read the full article ↓

The question. When we see only part of a system, what would make our account of it complete enough to predict what happens next? The retained report examines four parts of that problem: whether proposed mechanisms supply predictions, whether a compressed record retains enough information, whether separate observations fit one possible history, and which experiments can tell competing explanations apart.

Recovery status. The original technical report was recovered with its checksum intact. The original source inventory is retained privately and is not included here. The underlying source changes and complete machine-readable results were not recovered after an interrupted work session. This is an archival account of the reported findings, not a current independent reproduction. Earlier validation statements in the report remain historical; they do not establish a successful rerun in the recovered workspace.

Proposals still need prediction rules. The report identifies missing executable rules in the existing mechanism catalogue, leaving the proposed mechanisms without the inputs needed for a quantitative comparison. A neatly connected proposal does not establish that its physical assumptions are independent, and a missing prediction rule is not a failed physical theory. No new gravity equation was supplied to fill that gap.

A useful summary can still lose the next answer. In the finite systems described, two states could have the same visible average and still respond differently when control and environment changed together. Testing those influences one at a time missed that difference. The reported repair retained the hidden distinctions within the tested family. Combining averages also required the size of each group: averaging the averages alone could make the answer depend on the route taken.

Pairwise agreement can hide an impossible history. The reported examples show that matching each pair of observations was not enough to make all observations fit one shared classical preparation. The study recorded compatible histories and exact contradictions under its stated assumptions. Its scope is the finite classical models described; it does not solve every possible classical or quantum reconstruction problem.

Choosing a test did not produce a unique winner. The report compares a finite menu of experiments while accounting for selection, feedback and shared sources of uncertainty. Several designs tied. The policies selected the same tests, so the reported run did not establish that the more elaborate selection rule was superior. The selected experiments already distinguished the predictive behaviours in this finite example; the follow-up settings checked those predictions and added no further discrimination. A label that never affected predictions remained unidentified.

The retained account describes protocols and initial code frozen before the first computation, followed by a software-layout correction that reproduced the same four-study outcomes. That historical check is a rerun of known results, not another discovery. The missing execution records prevent independently verifying those steps from this recovered publication alone. October 13, 2026 No measurable stand-in for the feeling of seeing survives our own rules Basis: the phenomenal-observable study, program record of 2026-10-13. Read the full article ↓

The question. The program studies what seeing reports — that side is testable. What seeing feels like is fenced off, unless something measurable could stand in for the feeling without simply being the report renamed. This study asked what such a stand-in would have to look like for the program to adopt one, and scored the serious candidates against the answer.

The requirements. Five, each forced by a rule the program already runs on: a stated measuring procedure anyone can carry out; the quantity must not be report renamed; the link between the quantity and experience (the bridge) must be checkable against evidence that does not come from the quantity itself; the quantity must discriminate what experience is like, not only whether it is present; and it must fit inside the framework's scope.

What the candidates did. The level of consciousness — whether experience is present, and roughly how much — is genuinely measurable. The strongest measure (perturb the brain, and score how complex the echo is) passes the procedure requirement and is plainly not report renamed. But it reaches level and presence only. And every candidate failed the same requirement — the bridge. A bridge can only be checked against an anchor, and the only anchor anyone has is report: what people say they experienced. Where report exists, the stand-in adds nothing the program does not already test directly. Where report is absent — the unresponsive patient, the no-report trial, the non-human system, exactly the cases a stand-in would earn its keep — no check is possible, so no kill condition exists, and the claim cannot be sorted.

The verdict. No candidate survives. One leading candidate (a quantity computed from a system's causal structure) failed the procedure requirement at its own authors' statement. Nothing measurable anywhere in the survey touches phenomenal character — what experience is like, as opposed to whether it is there. This is a verdict about what can be adopted as a measurement — not a claim that experience is unreal, or that it is the same thing as a report.

What would change it. The study names its exits: a complexity measure taken while a patient cannot report, registered in advance to predict what the patient later reports on recovery, done to standard across cohorts; a structural prediction made away from the anchor; independent measures converging without recovery cases. Until one of those exists, the fence stands — by result, not by preference.

The line's audit found a consequential bookkeeping error. Study 018 rejected the pooled form of the record-redundancy idea — but the within-person contrast form was never the thing that study's verdict bore on, and the contrast measurement inside it was positive in all 22 subjects. The line had been carried as if the idea had failed wholesale. It had not: the contrast survives, unjudged, at even odds.

Study 027's failure was diagnosed properly. Its test asked whether the orientation of a faint stimulus could be read from scalp recordings — and orientation cannot be read from scalp recordings even when the stimulus is plainly visible. The arena failed before the claim could: only 1 of 40 recordings was usable at the standard fixed in advance.

The line therefore reopens with readability as its first problem. A pilot on a suitable dataset must show the perception signal can be read at all — a median per-subject reading accuracy of at least 0.60, across at least 30 subjects — before any claim test may be frozen. The dataset deposit for the pilot is authorized; the pilot measures readability only and takes no claim statistics.

Two further candidate forms — a no-report variant and a metacognition variant of the surviving claim — are staged for formulation. And the fence question was put and answered the same day: phenomenology stays out of scope, and the companion study (above) shows the program's own rules are what keep it there.

October 12, 2026 The whole history, re-examined Basis: the framework realignment and full-history audit, 2026-10-12. Read the full article ↓

The program adopted a stricter discipline for what counts as settled. A claim passes through five stages — created, checked, explained, accepted, digested — and creation plus checking alone never settles it.

Every headline result was broken into its parts: is the statement faithful? is the evidence verified? is the claim genuinely open? is the attribution clean? does it matter? Each part scored on its own, with its own falsifier. Digestion — explanation a named reader can follow and challenge — became first-class tracked work, and a result that is verified but unexplained is now flagged as the risk it is.

Checking and acceptance are tracked as two lanes that never merge on paper: machine-speed verification, and human-paced acceptance. Easily graded counts never stand in for understanding, and every verdict states what remains not understood.

The discipline was then applied backwards, to the whole history: every closed study re-examined, corrections made only as new dated records beside the old — frozen records and issued verdicts were never altered, and what cannot be fixed is labelled a historical limitation in the open. Two results of that audit are on this page: the consciousness contrast was found unjudged rather than failed, and the verdict tallies that once appeared here were retired.

The consciousness line

The dataset problem comes first: before any claim test is frozen, a pilot must show the perception signal can be read from the recordings at all, at a bar fixed in advance. Both pilots have now run, and both failed that bar. The first — all 56 people in a public deposit from a near-threshold seeing experiment — could read which of four screen locations a face had appeared at clearly in only 15 of the 56 people, and the typical clear reading fell short of the bar. The second pilot ran on a different deposit: brain recordings from 35 people in a near-threshold detection experiment in Madrid, where what faint stimulus appeared varied in two ways at once — its orientation, and which half of the screen it appeared on — and where every trial also carries the person's own report of seeing. That reading cleared the bar's floor in 20 of the 35 people (the gate required at least 30), and the typical clear reading was 0.5935 against the required 0.60. The signal is real — across everyone who could be scored, the typical reading sat above chance, and shuffled controls sat at chance — but it is not strong enough in enough people. Four of the 35 could not be scored at all, because the deposit's own files for them break the format the frozen method expects; the rules fixed in advance count them as not clearing, and the method was not bent to fit them. So the honest position is now stronger than "still looking": three public datasets have been examined as arenas for this claim — in the first, the stimulus content could not be read even when it was clearly visible; the second and third failed this gate — and no audited public arena currently works. The claim itself has never been computed in any of them and stays unjudged. A failed readability gate is a finding about a dataset and a reading task; it is not evidence about the claim, in either direction.

The onset question — answered

The question has been answered at the second asking. The successor study closed with every one of its eighteen examples complete — the largest size included — and its verdict is on the board above: removing the pair-flipping couplings moved the record's starting point earlier in 17 of 18 examples, more strongly at larger sizes, while substitute couplings from entirely different families, at the same count and the same total strength, held the starting point exactly. What holds the onset is the filled channel between pairs of spins, not the couplings that fill it. What remains open sits one layer down: why a filled channel sets the depth at all, whether its capacity is a count or a strength, and whether the deepening continues past the largest size run. No successor study is registered yet; when one is, it will appear here.

Program two — The epistemic ledger

The second program is about evidence itself. In most fields, a claim's credibility lives in people's heads and in the reputations of the people repeating it. The epistemic ledger gives a claim a public, revisable grade instead: a score that goes up when evidence survives testing and down when it fails, with the reasons for every change written next to it, in the open, where anyone can argue with them. A grade is never a verdict from authority — it is a running summary of what the evidence has done so far, and it is allowed to move.

The framework behind the ledger — why grades should move the way they do, and what keeps the bookkeeping honest — is published as a framing paper, and the ledger itself is maintained in a public repository (link below). The same grading discipline runs underneath Program one: it is how we keep score on our own claims, and it is why every result above is stated with its mechanism and its reasons attached, never as a bare count.

Framing paper published; the ledger itself is working software, not a sketch — a public prototype (version 0.11.1, October 2026) that stores every claim in an append-only record, automatically re-grades the claims a new fact touches along with everything that depends on them, and has completed measured evaluations — including a proof package in which a stronger local model cut a frozen downstream error score from 0.4637 to 0.3194 on identical prompts. The method is in active use across both programs.
